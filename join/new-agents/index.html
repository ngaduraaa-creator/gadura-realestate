Newly licensed · Queens and Long Island

What your first year actually costs

Licensing schools are good at getting you to the exam. Almost nothing is written for the week after you pass — when you need a sponsoring broker and nobody has told you what the year is going to cost.

Here is the floor, built from published schedules, with nothing rounded in our favour.

The verified floor

Every line below comes from a published fee schedule — the Department of State, the Long Island Board of REALTORS®, and OneKey® MLS. This is what you cannot avoid paying. It is deliberately not a budget: see what is missing, below.

Two things the national pages get wrong, which matter to your number. First, errors-and-omissions insurance is not required by New York State law. Several widely-read pages say it is. It is commonly required by the brokerage — which means it is a question for whoever you are joining, not a fixed cost of being licensed, and only a brokerage can tell you the answer. Second, LIBOR dues are pro-rated by the month you join, so the annual figure is not what you would pay on day one; the amount depends on when in the year you start. Ask for the pro-rated figure for your actual start month.
This is a floor, not a budget — and the gap matters. It deliberately excludes everything we could not price from a published schedule: your 77-hour qualifying course, errors-and-omissions insurance, anything your brokerage charges you, a lockbox device, signage, photography, business cards, a car, and the months before your first commission clears. We would rather give you one number we can stand behind than a confident-looking total that is half estimate.

The part recruiters tend to skip

You will see a figure circulated that says something like “87% of new agents fail in their first year.” We went looking for its source and there isn’t one — it appears in no NAR report and no study, only on coaching and recruiting blogs, and the claim mutates as it is passed along. We are not going to scare you with a number nobody can stand behind.

Here is a real one instead. In NAR’s 2026 Member Profile, median gross income was $8,000 for members with two years of experience or less, against $88,500 for those with sixteen years or more. That is a survey of NAR members who were still members when asked, so it cannot see anyone who already left — it understates the difficulty rather than overstating it.

And if you want an attrition figure that has actually been through peer review: a study in the Review of Financial Studies analysing 8.5 million listings and 567,230 distinct agents across 60 MLS platforms found that inexperienced agents exit at a rate near 30% a year, against under 5% for the most experienced. Separately, an analysis of state licence records in Wisconsin and Arizona found roughly half of licensees have let their licence lapse by year five or six.

Those are real numbers with real methods behind them, and they say something quite different from “87% fail in year one.” Attrition is steady and it is concentrated in the early years — not a cliff you either survive or do not.

What that means practically: plan for a long runway before commission income is dependable, and ask any brokerage what the first ninety days actually look like before you commit to one. The $1,136 above is what you owe before any of that starts.

The order things happen in

This trips people up constantly, including in online forums where it is answered wrongly.

  1. Complete the 77-hour qualifying course. New York requires it before you sit the exam.
  2. Pass the state exam.
  3. Find your sponsoring broker. You need one before your licence can be issued — the application requires your broker’s licence information, and the broker has to authorise it from their side. This is why “should I find a broker before or after the exam” has a real answer: you can interview whenever you like, but the licence does not exist until a broker signs on.
  4. Join an association and an MLS. Separate from your licence, with separate fees. Ask which ones your brokerage carries before you assume.

The Queens question nobody warns you about

If you are going to work in Queens, this one is worth more than any other piece of advice on this page.

Queens is served by two listing systems — the REBNY Residential Listing Service and OneKey® MLS — and you are required to join neither. Which one your brokerage pays for shapes which inventory you can see and list, and carrying both is a four-figure annual decision.

So ask, before you sign anywhere: which MLS do you pay for, do you cover the start-up fee, and what happens if my business crosses into the other one? A brokerage that cannot answer that clearly has told you something useful.

What to ask any brokerage — including us

A lot of new agents say the hardest part is not knowing which questions are the real ones. These are.

  • What will I pay you, and when — monthly, per transaction, or both? Get the full stack, not just the split.
  • Which MLS and which association, and who pays the start-up fees?
  • Who specifically will I work with in my first ninety days, and how often? Ask for a name.
  • What happens on my first deal — who reviews my contract before it goes out?
  • Do you take part-time agents? If you do, what does that actually look like here?
  • If I leave in a year, what comes with me and what stays? Our switching guide covers why that answer is almost always in your contract rather than in law.
