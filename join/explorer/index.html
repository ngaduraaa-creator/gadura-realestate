I am newly licensed

The licensing course ends at the exam. Almost nothing is written for the week after you pass, when you need a sponsoring broker and nobody has told you what the year costs.

Where are you in the process?

Finish the 77 hours first, then sit the exam. New York requires the qualifying course before you can test. Nothing else can start until that is done.

Worth knowing now so it does not surprise you later: you cannot be licensed without a sponsoring broker. The application needs the broker’s licence information and the broker authorises it from their side. You can interview whenever you like — but the licence does not exist until a broker signs on.

Your next step: Finish the course, then start interviewing sponsoring brokers before you pass rather than after. Take two questions to each: which MLS and which association do you carry, and do you cover the start-up fee? Check their answers against our itemised first-year costs.

This is the step the schools do not cover. You need a sponsoring broker before your licence is issued, not after. Interview more than one, and take the same questions to each.

The two that matter most and get asked least: which MLS and which association do you carry, and do you cover the start-up fee? In Queens that is a four-figure annual difference, because the borough is served by two systems and you are required to join neither.

The first-year cost breakdown has the line-by-line figures.

Your next step: Put the same two questions to every firm you interview and write the answers side by side. A firm that will not answer the fee question plainly has told you something.

Then you are past the hardest administrative part. The useful reading for you is what a first year actually costs once you are running — the cost page — and, if you are already weighing a move, how changing brokerages works in New York.

Your next step: Work out your own twelve-month floor from the cost page before you commit anywhere — add your own travel, phone and marketing to our published figures.

Your next step: Take the itemised floor, add your own travel, phone and marketing, and treat that total as the cash you need in hand before your first closing. Then ask each firm which of those lines they cover.

Fair worry, and the honest answer is to make every brokerage you talk to be specific. Ask who you would work with in your first ninety days, how often, and get a name. A vague answer should count against whoever gives it — including us.

We are not advertising a formal training programme, because we have not documented one. What we would say is: bring the question, and weigh our answer against what anyone else puts in writing.

Your next step: Ask every firm — this one included — who you would work with in your first ninety days, how often, and get a name. Compare what each puts in writing, and discount anyone who will not.

The most useful protection is knowing what you would lose if you left. That is almost entirely decided by the agreement you sign, not by law — so read it before you sign rather than after.

Our switching guide maps which of the common worries are settled by law, by your MLS, or by your contract. It is worth reading before your first brokerage, not just before your second.

Your next step: Before you sign anywhere, take the contract checklist to the firm and ask for the departure terms in writing. The answer is more informative than anything on a recruiting page.

I am an experienced agent

You already know how to do this job, so this will not explain real estate to you. What changes when you move is mostly operational, and most of it is decided by your agreement rather than by law.

What is prompting you to look?

That is the most common reason given, and it is worth testing specifically rather than generally. Ask any firm: who reviews my contract before it goes out, who answers at 9pm on a deal that is coming apart, and is that a named person or a queue?

At a firm our size there is no layer between you and the supervising broker. That is a structural fact about small independents rather than a programme we built — and it is the one thing we would ask you to compare honestly against where you are.

Your next step: Ask who reviews your contracts and who answers at 9pm on a deal coming apart — by name, at your current firm and at every firm you talk to. Then compare the two answers rather than the two pitches.

Then you should know our position before you spend an hour: we do not publish our commission terms on this website. Ask, and you get a straight answer from the supervising broker early in the conversation rather than after three meetings.

We will not pretend the split is a secondary concern — for most agents it is the deciding factor. If a published number is what you need in order to evaluate a firm at all, some brokerages do publish one, and choosing on that basis is entirely reasonable.

Your next step: Ask us for the number directly, early. If you need a published split in order to evaluate a firm at all, say so and we will tell you quickly whether to spend the hour.

Worth separating two things that often get bundled: what your brokerage requires, and what New York law and your MLS require. A fair amount of what feels like house policy is actually DOS or MLS rule, and it will follow you to the next firm.

Ask any brokerage which of its rules are its own. The answer tells you a lot.

Your next step: Write down the three rules that frustrate you most, then check in the guide which are DOS or MLS rules. Those follow you to the next firm, so only the rest are worth moving for.

Do you have active listings or deals in contract?

Then read the switching guide before you give notice. Three things matter most and surprise people:

You cannot move your own licence. Your current broker must terminate the association before any new firm can pick you up. That step belongs to the brokerage you are leaving.

Your listings sit with your current brokerage, not with you. Moving one needs your broker’s consent, your seller’s consent and fresh paperwork.

Commission on a deal in contract but not closed is governed by your agreement. We found no MLS or association rule that addresses it. Read that clause now rather than later.

Your next step: Pull your agreement today and read the commission-on-pending-deals clause before you give notice. That one clause decides more money than the split will this year.

Still worth reading the mechanics — particularly that your old broker has to release you before anyone can pick you up, and that your lockbox device is due back within five working days of the affiliation ending, or 48 hours if asked sooner. That rule binds you personally and is not in the vendor’s own documentation.

Your next step: Ask your broker for the release in writing, and diarise the lockbox return the day the affiliation ends — five working days, or 48 hours if asked sooner.

Then the move is administratively simpler than most, and the questions worth asking shift to what you are joining rather than what you are leaving. The experienced-agent page covers what actually changes operationally.

Your next step: Read the experienced-agent page and bring the questions it raises to a first conversation. With nothing pending, the only question left is what you are joining.

I am considering switching brokerages

The single most useful thing to understand before you give notice: in New York you cannot move your own licence. Your current broker has to release you first, and only then can your next broker pick you up.

How far along are you?

Good order to do it in. Start with what the move actually involves — it is sourced from the Department of State, the OneKey® rulebook and REBNY’s published rules, and it is useful whoever you end up joining.

The finding that surprised us most: the public MLS rulebooks are largely silent about the departing salesperson. Of five questions we put to that layer, it squarely answers one. Almost everything else is your contract.

Your next step: Read the guide and check its citations against the Department of State and OneKey® yourself. Whoever you join, you will negotiate better for having done it.

Then the order of operations matters. Before you give notice, pull your agreement and find the answers to: commission on deals in contract, who releases your listings and on what timetable, and whose name is on your database, CRM, email and domains.

The guide has the full list of questions and shows which are settled by law versus by that agreement.

Your next step: Pull your agreement and answer those three questions — pending commission, listing release, database ownership — before you give notice, in that order.

Then two deadlines matter immediately. Your lockbox device is due back within five working days of the affiliation ending, or 48 hours if they ask sooner — that binds you personally and is not in the vendor FAQ. And your MLS and association memberships do not travel with the licence change; they are separate, with separate fees.

If the release itself is being slow-walked, that is worth raising early — nothing in the licensing process routes around your old broker’s step.

Your next step: Diarise the lockbox deadline now, and start the MLS and association transfers in parallel with the licence change rather than after it, so you are not inactive for a fortnight.

What are you most worried about losing?

Listings sit with the brokerage, not the agent. Moving one needs your broker’s consent, the seller’s consent, and new paperwork at the receiving firm. Published guidance from the state REALTOR® association puts it plainly: listings belong to the broker and transfer only with the broker’s agreement.

Ask any firm you are considering what their agreement says about listings on departure. A brokerage that will put that in writing is telling you something useful.

Your next step: Ask every firm you are considering for its departure terms on listings in writing, before you sign anything. Then read your current agreement’s clause on the same point.

Exclusive agreements are with the brokerage. A release is possible, but it is your broker’s decision and the client has to agree too.

One trap: if you read national or NYC advice, REBNY’s rules contain a detailed “former exclusive agent” regime — and it is NYC-only. If you work in Queens or Long Island under OneKey, it does not govern you. Advice written for the wrong system is worse than none.

Your next step: Confirm which system actually governs you — OneKey® or REBNY — before you act on any advice you have read. If you are in Queens or on Long Island, most NYC guidance does not apply to you.

This one has a regulation behind it and it is stricter than most agents expect. A New York regulation requires a salesperson on termination to turn over to the broker all listing information obtained during the association — including information you acquired yourself while affiliated, not only what the broker handed you.

We cite that as it appears in the state association’s own publication rather than from the regulation text, so treat it as a strong signal to get specific advice. Whose name is on your CRM, your email address and your domains is a contract question, and worth settling in writing before you build a brand on an address you do not control.

Your next step: Check today whose name is on your CRM account, your email address and your domain. Move anything you own onto an address you control before you give notice, and get specific advice on the listing-information rule.

I lead a team

You are the most consequential reader on this site and the one we can say least to in public, because team structures and broker-level arrangements turn on terms that are not settled in advance.

What we can state precisely is the process.

Roughly how many agents would move with you?

Small enough that the conversation is mostly operational: who reviews contracts when several deals land in a week, what happens to each agent’s pipeline during the transfer, and how the association and MLS changes are sequenced so nobody is unlicensed for a fortnight.

Those are answerable in one conversation. The economics are answerable in the same one.

Your next step: Bring your team’s structure to a first conversation and we will sequence the transfer so nobody is unlicensed mid-move. Ask for the economics in the same conversation.

At that size capacity is the honest question, and you should ask it bluntly: can this office absorb the transaction volume, and who specifically does the work when it arrives? We would rather answer that directly than have you discover the ceiling afterwards.

Bring the volume figures you are comfortable sharing and we will tell you what is realistic.

Your next step: Bring the volume figures you are comfortable sharing to a first call. We will tell you what this office can absorb before you move, not after.

Then be aware you are talking to a small independent brokerage, and that is a real constraint rather than a modest one. The useful first conversation is whether the shape of what you want is possible here at all — and a straight no is a legitimate outcome we will give you quickly rather than slowly.

Your next step: Send the shape of what you need before you invest a meeting in it. If it is not possible here, you will get a straight no quickly — which is worth more to you than a slow maybe.

How a team conversation runs here. You and the supervising broker — no recruiter, no regional layer, nobody taking notes to pass upward. What gets decided on the call: whether what you want is possible here, and what the economics would look like. What waits for paperwork: anything binding. Team structures get written down and reviewed before either side relies on them.
