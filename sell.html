The first conversation

What happens in a valuation conversation

Most sellers start by talking the property through with a licensed agent. Nothing is signed until you decide to list.

  1. You get in touch

    Call or email the office with the property address, the type of property and what you are trying to decide.

  2. Agency disclosure

    Before discussing details, the agent gives you the New York agency disclosure form and explains whom they would represent.

  3. A look at the property

    The agent reviews condition, layout, updates and legal use (such as the certificate of occupancy) and, for a co-op or condo, the building’s rules and monthly charges.

  4. Comparable sales

    The agent prepares a comparative market analysis (CMA): recent sales of similar nearby properties, adjusted for differences. A CMA is an opinion of value, not an appraisal.

  5. Costs and net proceeds

    You go through the likely selling costs and your mortgage payoff to see an estimated net figure. Your real estate attorney confirms the final numbers.

  6. Your decision

    You decide whether to list now, wait, or not sell. If you list, you sign a listing agreement with the brokerage. Commission is negotiable and is not set by law.

No sale price or timeline is promised. A CMA and any estimate reflect the information available when they are prepared.

Before you call

What to have ready

You do not need everything for a first conversation. These documents answer most of the questions buyers and attorneys ask later.

Every property

  • Deed, or details of how title is held
  • Latest mortgage statement, and any home-equity line
  • Recent property tax bills (and, in New York City, the water and sewer bill)
  • Certificate of occupancy, permits, and any open violations you know of
  • List of repairs and improvements, with dates and receipts or warranties
  • Known issues with the property. New York’s Property Condition Disclosure Act applies to most one- to four-family sales; ask your attorney what you must give the buyer.

Two- to four-family homes

  • Current leases and rent amounts
  • Security deposits held
  • Which units, if any, will be vacant at closing

Co-op apartments

  • Stock certificate and proprietary lease
  • Recent maintenance statement
  • The building’s sale requirements from the managing agent: board application, any transfer fee or flip tax, and sublet rules
  • Offering plan and recent building financial statements, if you have them

Condominium apartments

  • Unit deed
  • Declaration, bylaws or offering plan
  • Recent common charges and tax statements
  • Managing agent’s contact details, for the buyer’s questionnaire

Selling for an estate

  • Letters testamentary or letters of administration
  • Your estate attorney’s contact details

Price, timing, net proceeds

What affects your price, your timing and what you keep

Condition and presentation

Repairs, cleanliness, layout and photographs affect how buyers compare your home with others.

Comparable sales

Recent sales of similar properties nearby, not asking prices, are the main evidence for a realistic price.

Market conditions

Interest rates, the number of competing listings and the season change over time. The agent explains what recent sales show when you talk.

Co-op boards and buyer financing

A co-op board usually reviews each buyer, and many buildings set financing rules. Board review and the buyer’s mortgage approval both affect timing and who can buy.

Paperwork and legal use

A certificate of occupancy that does not match the use, open permits, tenants or estate paperwork can delay a sale. Raising them early helps.

Your own timing

When you need to move, where you are going next, and whether you are buying at the same time all shape the plan.

Closing costs and net proceeds

Typical seller costs include the brokerage commission (negotiable, not set by law), New York State real estate transfer tax, New York City real property transfer tax for Queens properties, attorney fees, recording costs, and any co-op or condo fees, such as a co-op flip tax. Any mortgage is normally paid off at closing from the sale proceeds. Rates and thresholds change, so this page does not quote them. Seller questions

Common questions about selling

Is the comparative market analysis free, and is it an appraisal?

There is no charge for the conversation or the comparative market analysis (CMA), and no obligation to list. A CMA is a licensed agent’s opinion of value based on recent sales of similar properties. It is not an appraisal. If you need a formal appraisal, for example for a court or an estate, use a New York State licensed or certified appraiser. A buyer’s lender arranges its own appraisal.

Who would I be working with?

Gadura Real Estate LLC is the licensed brokerage, and a listing agreement would be with the brokerage. A licensed real estate salesperson, such as Nitin Gadura, or the supervising broker, Vinod K. Gadura, works with you. You can check any New York real estate license on the Department of State’s public license lookup.

How long will it take to sell?

It depends on the property, its condition, the price, the market at the time and, for a co-op or condo, the building’s approval process. The agent can explain what recent comparable sales suggest, but no timeline is promised.

What will it cost to sell?

Typical seller costs include the brokerage commission, which is negotiable and not set by law; New York State real estate transfer tax; New York City real property transfer tax for properties in Queens; your attorney’s fee; and, for a co-op or condo, any building fees, such as a co-op flip tax. Any mortgage is normally paid off at closing from the sale proceeds. Rates and thresholds change, so this page does not quote them: the New York State Department of Taxation and Finance and the New York City Department of Finance publish the current transfer tax rates, and your attorney confirms every figure.

Do I have to fix up my home before selling?

Not necessarily. Some owners make small repairs or clean and declutter; others sell as-is. The agent can talk through what buyers are likely to notice, but the decision and the cost are yours.

Can you help with a co-op, a condo or a two- to four-family home?

Yes. A co-op sale usually needs the building board’s approval of the buyer and follows the managing agent’s requirements. A condo sale usually involves a building questionnaire and may involve the board’s right of first refusal. A two- to four-family sale with tenants involves leases, rents, security deposits and which units will be vacant at closing. The checklist on this page lists the documents for each.

What is the New York agency disclosure form?

For a residential sale, New York Real Property Law §443 requires a real estate licensee acting as an agent to give you an agency disclosure form at the first substantive contact. The form explains whom the agent represents, the seller or the buyer, and that one agent may represent both only as a dual agent, with the informed written consent of both.
